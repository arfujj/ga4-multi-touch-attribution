Key findings

  1. Organic Search is worth more than last-click reporting shows. Both data-driven models give Organic Search more revenue credit than last touch: $109,173 → $126,801 (Markov, +16.1%) and $120,953 (Shapley, +10.8%); both 95% intervals exclude zero. It opens 41.6% of converting journeys but closes only 34.3%. Action: Judge SEO and content on data-driven or position-based credit, and don't cut organic investment based on last-click numbers.
  2. Direct gets too much credit, and half of it is really missing tracking. Direct loses revenue credit under both data-driven models (-4.7% Markov, -13.6% Shapley; both significant). 50% of Direct's 188,830 sessions carry no source information at all, and under last non-direct click its share of conversions falls from 35.8% to 18.3%. Action: Fix tracking first: UTM-tag every email and partner link, configure cross-domain measurement and unwanted-referral exclusions, then re-run this analysis.
  3. Paid Search assists more sales than it gets credit for, but each visit is worth the least. Paid Search is the only channel both data-driven models agree last touch under-credits on conversions: 47 → 62.4 (Markov, +32.9%) and 61.1 (Shapley, +29.9%). Even so, it earns $0.33/session vs $1.23 for Organic Search and $2.55 for Referral (Markov). Action: Report paid search on data-driven conversions, then compare cost per data-driven conversion to the other channels before changing its budget.
  4. Moving budget toward the "under-credited" channel would lose money here. Shifting 10% of Email effort (35 sessions) to Organic Search changes data-driven revenue by -$114 over 92 days (95% CI -$208 to -$45), even with constant returns. Email still earns $4.48/session vs $1.23. Action: Protect Email. Use the attribution results to re-rank channels, then make reallocation decisions with cost data and a holdout test.
  5. The biggest leak is the product page, not checkout, and it isn't a mobile problem. Only 19.7% of sessions that view a product add it to the cart (61,847 of 77,020 sessions drop). Once checkout starts, 64.7% add payment info and 73.8% of those buy. Desktop (19.6%) and mobile (19.9%) add to cart at the same rate (p = 0.28). Action: A/B test the product page: price and shipping clarity, stock and size information, and the prominence of the add-to-cart button.

Limitations

  • Obfuscated sample data. 26.3% of sessions have no source and are treated as Direct (GA4's convention), and 8.8% fall in Other/Unknown. Google also masks some values as `<Other>` or `(data deleted)`.
  • Short window. 92 days (2020-11-01 to 2021-01-31), spanning the holidays. Journeys that started before November are cut off, and users who would have bought after January are counted as non-converting.
  • Attribution is correlational. Every model, including the data-driven ones, redistributes credit for sales that happened. None measures what would have happened without a channel. Only holdout or geo experiments can show incrementality.
  • No cost or cross-device data. Effort is proxied by sessions, and journeys are per browser (`user_pseudo_id`), so people who switch devices look like separate users.
